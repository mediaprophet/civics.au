Opportunities / Community IT support

A community site needs people who can run it.

A community microgrid or digital cooperative cannot run on fly-in contractors. It needs people on the ground who can install, maintain and explain it—and the same skills answer another local problem: residents locked out of the digital world by cost, confidence or circumstance.

Two needs, one skills base

A ground running solar, storage, public Wi-Fi, edge databoxes or a community datacentre carries a permanent technical workload: installations, fault calls, firmware, network changes, safety systems and user help. Outsourcing it to distant contractors is slow, expensive and sends the capability—and the wages—somewhere else.

The region around the site holds the mirror problem. Residents need affordable help with the technology daily life now assumes: pensioners priced out of a service call, families re-establishing after separation, people with disability configuring assistive devices, anyone whose work, health or banking moved online without them. Commercial tech support is priced for commercial customers; charity means someone skilled gives up a Saturday for a cup of tea.

The same program answers both. Every technician the site needs is a resident who can be trained; every resident who needs support is a reason to train them. What began as a seniors' IT-support pilot concept is reframed here as the workforce arm of the community site itself.

The capability loop

1

Demand is defined by the site

The build specifies the work: racking a databox, configuring VLANs, maintaining public-access Wi-Fi, checking sensors, helping users. The site planner's microgrid and digital cooperative scenarios list the systems a ground actually runs—each line item is effectively a job description.

2

Training is modular and supervised

Short courses paired with supervised fieldwork, delivered online and on-site. People who already have the skills skip ahead—recognition of prior learning converts existing competence into the same credential format rather than forcing everyone through the same course.

3

Supported delivery comes first

New technicians serve residents under supervision—seniors, people in crisis, people with special needs—where patience and trustworthiness matter more than speed. It is real work with real stakes, and the safest classroom that exists.

4

Reputation accumulates

Every job returns structured feedback; credentials and references accumulate into a portable work record the person owns. For someone whose employment history is thin, damaged or unverifiable, that record is the way back into paid work—often the first step of the journey out.

Who the service is for

Affordability is assessed on merit: concession rates are unlocked by a concession credential, community organisations can issue service vouchers to people in crisis, and full-rate work subsidises the supported kind. The situations below are the design cases, not an exhaustive list.

Support means access to a person's online life. Working-with-vulnerable-people checks, police checks where warranted, supervision and clear job scope are part of the service definition—not optional overhead.

Three training tracks

Residential & supported services

Home connectivity assessment, device setup and maintenance habits, safe-account practices, assistive and safety technology. Special-situation flags—elderly, regional or remote, low digital confidence, specialised equipment, domestic-violence-related work—attach extra protocol requirements.

Site & network operations

Business-grade connectivity enablement, VLAN-aware switching, public-access Wi-Fi, multi-user network security, failover and uptime—the workload a ground's shared infrastructure generates daily.

Edge & cooperative systems

IoT sensors, energy monitoring, databoxes, and the racks and AI workloads the digital cooperative runs—feeding directly into the site's own operating crew.

Recognition of prior learning

People who already hold the skills are credentialed on evidence rather than re-taught—customer, professional and training-provider references all count.

Credentials, checks and reputation

Each completed module or endorsed job issues a W3C verifiable credential (Open Badges-compatible) the person holds—not a platform login that disappears if the service folds. Employment-relevant evidence accumulates: training modules, supervised-job endorsements, customer feedback, police and working-with-vulnerable-people checks. For unemployed and under-employed participants this is the point of the exercise: a work history that can be presented to any employer and verified without ringing anyone.

The pieces already exist elsewhere in this stack: the concession credentials prove circumstances without exposing identity; the databox delivers training records, job outcomes and references to the person rather than to a platform. This program is the workload that gives those components something to carry.

Honest limit. A credential evidences training and recorded feedback, not guaranteed competence. Supervision, scoped job definitions and the willingness to retrain or de-credential on bad feedback carry the remaining risk.

How it sustains itself

  • Means-tested access: concession rates unlock with a credential; community service organisations issue prepaid vouchers to people in crisis.
  • Enterprise accounts: ISPs, telcos and electronics retailers buy last-mile install and support capacity in the region instead of flying technicians in—their margin on a service call is the program's income.
  • Prepaid and held in escrow: jobs are paid up front, released on completion with a review step; disputes have somewhere to go. BPAY and prepaid cards matter—this customer base does not all have credit cards.
  • A coordination margin, not a rent: the platform takes enough to run billing, checks and dispute handling; it is not a commercial marketplace extracting maximum commission.
  • The cooperative subsidises what cannot pay: the digital cooperative scenario already models selling spare capacity—hosting, compute, energy—outside the town. Part of that margin can fund service delivery to residents who cannot pay at all, which is precisely the cross-subsidy the site exists to demonstrate.

What a pilot looks like

Choose a ground already running a microgrid or digital cooperative build and take its support load from the modelling workbench outputs—always-on systems, public-safety workloads, connectivity. Recruit the first cohort from people already there: unemployed or under-employed tech-capable residents, volunteers, people rebuilding after disruption. Start with the residential and supported-services track—seniors are the clearest need and the most instructive classroom—then grow into site operations as the build proceeds.

Partners are a category, not a fixed roster: the local council, community service organisations, a telco or ISP, a training provider, seniors' and disability groups. The original concept named Victorian pilot supporters; the design is deliberately portable—any Australian community with a site, a need and a few underemployed capable people can run the same loop.
