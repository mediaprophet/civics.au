02 / Fit-out & budget

Work out the setup—and whether the numbers hold.

This planner is for people with little money to work with—low or constrained incomes, or a recent financial shock such as separation, job loss or disaster—who can't afford traditional housing. It prices the affordable end: serviceable second-hand rigs and modest fit-outs that make a dignified, legal home on wheels possible. Premium builds can be far better and far more expensive; they're welcome here, but they're not the focus. The real question the tool answers: can the vehicle legally carry or tow it, does it fit the people living in it, and does the weekly budget hold?

Five ways in

Who this is for. People working with tight means—after a financial disaster, on a constrained income, or priced out of traditional housing. The defaults and price ranges target serviceable second-hand gear, not premium builds; a new motorhome can be a better home, but it isn't what this tool is for. If you already have a rig—of any standard—plug it in; the checks work the same.

There is no single right answer—it depends on what you already have, who is travelling, and how much room you need. Each path has a different entry price and a different failure mode worth checking before money changes hands.

The fit-out & budget planner

Work through the six steps. Choosing a setup type fills in typical figures—adjust any of them to match an actual vehicle or van you are considering. Everything updates live and nothing is stored or sent anywhere.

Two futures — today vs Community Grounds

The steps below model the world as it is. This block holds the assumptions for the supported world: a community ground offering a powered site at a set charge — likely below commercial site fees — where a digital concession card proves eligibility for the concession rate. The rig and its equipment are still bought and financed by the owner — a powered ground simply means less of that equipment may be needed at all (tick what). And the set charge can sit below commercial fees because a ground's income isn't only camping fees — power, connectivity, hosted services and site amenity all contribute (see community grounds).

Stays work differently too: where powered sites and council or showground camping commonly cap stays at 7, 14 or 28 days, a ground's default stay cap is ~90 days — an order of magnitude longer — supporting a nomadic rhythm of moving between grounds while pitching in on projects or short-term work at each place. The cap flexes with circumstance: a seasonal worker might stay ~6 months near a job, and someone recently separated may need to stay put for years near their children — special consideration, and reasonable too, since their situation depends on them being there. The results compare both futures side by side.

Step 1 — Choose the setup

Many of the cheapest—and most remarkable—camps sit in state forests, farm stays and station country that a 2WD or low-clearance rig can't reach: river crossings, dirt tracks, muddy paddocks, corrugated or sandy roads, and routes cut by flood events. A 4WD-capable rig widens those options considerably. But capability has real costs—purchase price, tyres and suspension, more fuel on rough tracks, more wear—which can offset what you save on per-night camping fees at accessible places. And "4WD" isn't one thing: clearance, tyres, suspension, underbody protection, low-range gearing and water-crossing prep all set different limits, so match the rig to the tracks you actually intend to drive.

Weight check — does it fit the payload?

Everything you add—people, water, solar, gear—comes out of the vehicle's payload. Allow ~80 kg per person.

Weight check — can the vehicle tow it?

From the compliance plates and handbook: the trailer's ATM must not exceed the vehicle's rated tow capacity, and the ball weight must not exceed the towball rating. The trailer's payload (ATM − tare) must hold your gear and water.

Reading the numbers

The weight terms

  • Tare: what the trailer or van weighs empty, from the compliance plate.
  • ATM: the most a trailer may weigh fully loaded—people and gear included.
  • Ball weight: the downward force on the towball, usually 8–12% of loaded weight, capped by the vehicle's towball rating.
  • Tow capacity: the maximum braked trailer mass the vehicle may legally tow.
  • Payload: GVM minus kerb weight—everything the vehicle itself may carry.

With or without a ground

The planner compares your chosen accommodation scenario against the alternative benchmark: a community ground at the $15/night concession tariff versus commercial caravan-park rates. On a $600/week concession income the difference is roughly $245/week—the gap between a budget that holds and one that doesn't. Free camping is cheaper still but carries no amenities, no security of tenure and frequent moves, which is precisely the instability the grounds are designed to relieve.

Estimates, not approvals. This planner checks arithmetic, not legality. Before buying, verify the actual compliance-plate figures (tare, ATM, GVM, tow and ball ratings), get a weighbridge ticket, and confirm your licence class and insurance cover the combination.

Beyond the budget

Money and weight are the easy checks. The harder questions are whether mobile living suits the people involved—health, medications, schooling, pets, accessibility, and how far you'll be from support networks—and whether a particular second-hand vehicle is sound enough to trust. That's why the Walkabout Strategy puts suitability and independent inspection before purchase, and why a community ground matters: somewhere legal, connected and affordable to actually live while you stabilise. Proving eligibility for the concession tariff without a fixed address is where the concession credentials component comes in.

No product is being sold here. No financing product, lender, dealer or park arrangement is established or endorsed by this page. It's a planning aid to help people arrive at real conversations with better numbers.
