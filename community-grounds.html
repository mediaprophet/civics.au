The proposition

Rather than treating alternative housing as a systemic failure, we are establishing an innovative, dignified framework for modern nomadic living and transition. Developed regions increasingly rely on a mobile workforce to fill critical seasonal and skilled employment gaps; however, current urban modernisation efforts often marginalise or criminalise alternative living. By shifting public focus away from perpetual crisis management toward catalytic asset creation, we aim to reduce public expense and drastically improve quality of life, empowering capable individuals to transition from survival mode to productive, autonomous living.

Supporting milestones, not just mobility

Beyond simply providing a place to park, true autonomy requires space, dignity, and stability for fundamental human life stages:

  • A structural stepping stone for study: provides an accessible launchpad for young adults who wish to pursue vocational training, university degrees, or technical apprenticeships but are priced out by crushing urban rental markets.
  • Dignified foundation for raising children: creates an affordable, secure environment for young people and families choosing to have and raise children, offering community stability without the burden of unserviceable 30-year housing debt.
  • Cognitive agency & financial bandwidth: capping foundational living costs restores the mental clarity and financial headroom necessary for individuals and families to plan their long-term futures rather than expending all their energy on day-to-day survival.

Protecting the grassroots innovator

A critical flaw in modern economic development is the stark disparity between capital and creation: physical infrastructure and government grants are frequently funded, while the grassroots innovators utilising them often live below the poverty line, taking on crushing personal debts while attempting to build an economic future for themselves and their communities.

  • Transition from survival to focused enterprise: by eliminating predatory overheads, creators can focus sustained time, creative energy, and trade craft on building tangible civics solutions and small ventures.
  • Rejecting the extractive "start-up hub" model: our framework deliberately rejects conventional incubator dynamics, which too often expose unprotected creators to opportunistic vendors, corporate middlemen, and venture scouts seeking to harvest ideas from founders who cannot afford costly litigation to defend their intellectual property.
  • Protection of creators' moral & material interests: rooted in the fundamental human right to the protection of moral and material interests in one's creations (UDHR Art. 27), decentralised, W3C Solid-interoperable data pods and verifiable git/SVN-like resource commits ensure creators retain direct ownership and provenance over their work across diverse technologies, ensuring grassroots innovation directly advances the creator's own economic security rather than being captured by well-funded intermediaries.

Generative infrastructure, not an extractive toll-booth

Traditional housing and commercial caravan parks operate on an extractive landlord model, treating residents as the primary financial engine expected to bear capital debt and overheads through high fees. Community grounds fundamentally invert this dynamic. The site sustains itself through generative physical and digital infrastructure—commercial-scale rooftop solar, battery grid arbitrage, and edge compute databoxes. Because the infrastructure produces independent revenue streams, the ground does not need to extract high rents from people in transition. Operating overheads are decoupled from resident fees, allowing the site to offer a guaranteed, non-extractive $15 per night ($105/week) concession floor for essential shelter.

Bridging the infrastructure gap

While many regional show-grounds already feature foundational infrastructure—such as large sheds, public toilets, sports facilities, and existing electrical connections—they frequently lack the dedicated amenities and energy capacity required to support transitional living. Currently, there is no normative framework to employ and advance the use of this infrastructure to facilitate this purpose. To bridge this gap, our strategic plan:

  • Conducts tailored site assessments: evaluating structural capacity, electrical loads, and planning considerations for every candidate ground.
  • Upgrades core utilities: deploying commercial-scale solar, battery storage, and upgraded ablution blocks to transform day-use facilities into functional living spaces.
  • Builds disaster resilience: establishing islandable microgrids that provide emergency power, water access, and communications during disruptions.
  • Fosters local enterprise & fills workforce gaps: providing spaces where individuals can study, retrain, work remotely, or maintain vehicles without crushing overhead costs.

Keep the ground a community asset. Shows, sport, festivals and local organisations remain part of its core purpose. Transitional accommodation works alongside these traditions, supported by clear scheduling protocols during major event periods.

A local blueprint for global development

By perfecting this model domestically, we are creating a deployable framework that directly supports international Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Establishing decentralised utility networks and digital transformation in regional Australia serves as a practical blueprint for bringing vital infrastructure to developing regions worldwide facing acute energy, clean water, and connectivity deficits.

  • High-bandwidth digital hubs: aligning with international efforts like UNICEF's Project Connect by treating community grounds as digital access and education hubs.
  • The right to privacy & informational self-determination: leveraging open, W3C Solid-compatible standards to uphold the fundamental human right to privacy and informational self-determination, ensuring residents maintain personal autonomy over portable data pods across heterogeneous systems while seamlessly accessing local microgrids and services.
  • Targeted SDG delivery: creating verifiable outcomes for affordable clean energy (SDG 7), industry, innovation & infrastructure (SDG 9), and resilient communities (SDG 11).

What a ground could provide

01

Everyday essentials & ablutions

Upgraded ablution blocks, drinking water, modern laundry, reliable wastewater management and high-speed connectivity, designed for dignity, accessibility and personal privacy.

02

Community-scale renewable energy

Commercial-scale rooftop solar, community battery storage, and smart IoT metering that enables dynamic peer-to-peer energy matching between local generation and site demand—reducing diesel generator reliance and powering both events and medium-term stays.

03

Community maintenance & project hubs

Multi-purpose facilities and shared workshops where residents and locals can study, work remotely, share skills, and maintain vehicles to keep setups roadworthy and safe.

04

Designated mobile home bays

Engineered vehicle access, utility hookups, generous spacing, and clear stay agreements that ensure dwellings and the site operate together harmoniously.

Make the local arrangements work

Delivering community grounds shifts economic focus from recurring crisis management to catalytic asset creation, combining public and philanthropic co-investment with self-sustaining local operations.

  • Land and buildings: partner with local councils, agricultural societies and community ground trusts who steward the land and facilities.
  • Tiered pricing & cross-subsidisation: implement a tiered fee schedule where full-fee visitors and commercial events cross-subsidise the $15/night concession floor, reinforced by local solar energy sales.
  • Agreements & co-investment: establish clear roof-space leases, shared solar asset governance, and equitable distribution of generated energy and cost savings.
  • Local operations: deploy transparent management for site stays, maintenance, utility contributions and community spaces.
  • Community participation: ensure residents and local organisations have a voice in site stewardship, voluntary initiatives and shared rules.
  • Site feasibility: complete tailored engineering, zoning, bushfire/flood risk, and grid connection assessments for every candidate site before launch.

What to learn from a first ground

A flagship demonstration ground will validate how foundational upgrades, mobile living and shared energy work in practice. The initiative focuses on capable individuals facing life transitions—such as financial hardship, relationship separation or seeking an intentional nomadic lifestyle—who have modest income, desire autonomy, and benefit from an empowering strategic reset rather than institutional dependency.

For residents

Does the site provide predictable, low-cost living for concession card holders (at the $15/night electronic tariff within a ~$600/week baseline)? Is the $15 floor fully protective even during severe illness? Are amenities and microgrids dependable? Does autonomy and stability allow people to plan their next chapter with dignity?

For the host town

Do local businesses, clubs and annual shows gain from enhanced facilities and reliable clean energy? Does the asset-building model deliver lasting local value?
