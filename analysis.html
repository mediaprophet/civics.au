Community planning environment

Plan a stronger future together.

This is the doorway to the advanced planning environment built for Community Grounds. It helps a town explore a real proposal, test practical choices and show how the work could improve life for people and the wider community.

Intentions, assumptions & sources ↗

A planning room for the community

Big decisions are often made with a pile of reports, a few spreadsheets and a meeting where no one can see how the pieces fit together. This environment is being built to bring the practical questions into one place: the ground, the people, the local economy, energy and water, operating costs, possible income, community benefit and the things that still need to be checked.

Start with a real place

Set out the ground, the facilities it already has, the services it needs and the work required to make it welcoming and useful.

Test choices together

Compare sensible options: a small first stage, a larger build, different energy choices, different ways of running the site and different local partnerships.

Bring evidence into the picture

Use public information and local knowledge to understand housing pressure, seasonal work, learning, tourism, services and community opportunity.

Produce a clear proposal

Turn the discussion into a report that shows the costs, benefits, assumptions, unanswered questions and reasons for the next decision.

Open the planning environment ↗

The purpose is not to let a machine make a community decision. It gives people a clearer way to see the consequences of different choices, ask better questions and make the decision together.

What better looks like

This is about the kind of difference a well-run Community Ground can make. It is not about asking people to prove that they are struggling, and it is not about making a computer decide who deserves help.

A safe place to land

A person who has had a difficult time has somewhere calm, practical and affordable to stay while they work out their next step.

A chance to contribute

People can share skills, learn, make things, perform, help with events or lend a hand in ways that are useful to the town.

More breathing room for local services

Community and public housing can focus on people with the most urgent and complex needs, while others have another dignified pathway.

A stronger town

Local shops, clubs, venues, service providers and community groups can welcome people who bring energy, skills and custom.

The questions that matter

Before a community starts a pilot, it should be able to answer a few plain questions.

Is there a good place for it?

Does the town have a suitable ground, willing partners, safe access, basic amenities and a way to run it well?

Who could it help?

Students, seasonal workers, people rebuilding after a relationship breakdown or failed business, artists, carers, retirees, people changing direction, and others looking for a purposeful next chapter may all have different needs.

What could the town gain?

More visitors at quiet times, a musician at the pub, help for a local event, new small businesses, practical skills and stronger local connections.

What must be in place first?

Clear rules, kind but firm management, accessible facilities, local support links, safe transport, reliable services and a plan for problems before they happen.

Learning from the bigger picture

We have gathered public information about housing, homelessness services, work, education, tourism, local business, retirement and safety. It helps a town ask better questions and see where pressure or opportunity may exist.

Public figures describe broad patterns. They do not tell us a person's story, predict who will come, or decide what is right for a community.

How we will keep the work honest

  1. Listen first.Residents, local businesses, service providers, Traditional Owners, councils and community groups help define the question before a plan is written.
  2. Show the assumptions.Costs, likely income, expected use and possible benefits are written down so people can challenge or improve them.
  3. Keep different kinds of value separate.What a site costs to run, what it may save government, and what it means for people's lives are related but not the same thing.
  4. Say what is not known.Uncertain figures stay uncertain. A hopeful idea is not presented as a proven result.
  5. Learn from a small first step.A pilot should make it possible to improve the plan, change course or stop if it is not working.

Good record keeping stays in the background

People should see the outcome, not be asked to understand the machinery behind it. The work still needs careful records so that a council, funder or community can check where a claim came from and how a figure was reached.

The project uses its own Web Civics version of QualiaDB as behind-the-scenes infrastructure for this purpose. It helps keep source material, definitions and working notes organised for this and related projects. The public offer is the community benefit and a clear account of the evidence; the underlying QualiaDB work is not being given away as the product.

Next step: choose a willing community and work through these questions together. The planning tools can then help turn local information into a clear, reviewable first-stage proposal.
