In plain terms / The cooperative

A knowledge bank, run like a credit union.

A knowledge bank cooperative is a neighbourhood institution for the digital age: it keeps a community’s information in the community, under the community’s own laws, with the people who run it bound to serve the members—never the other way round.

The cooperative runs the shared parts

A credit union pools a town's savings so the town benefits instead of a distant bank. A knowledge bank cooperative does the same for information: it runs the shared machinery—storage, connections, software updates, and data safes for people who do not want a box at home—while each member keeps authority over their own records. Members, not customers.

People who owe you a duty

The professionals who maintain the cooperative act under what the design calls an informatics fiduciary duty—the same kind of obligation a lawyer owes a client or a doctor owes a patient: strict loyalty to your interests, confidentiality, and no profiting from what they hold for you. It is a duty that can be enforced, not a slogan. And the software they run for you is a sub-agent—an obedient tool executing instructions, never an autonomous decision-maker.

Local law, not foreign fine print

Most services Australians use are governed by contracts written under foreign law—very often the courts of California, or wherever the company's head office sits. If something goes wrong, your remedy is overseas: expensive, slow, and in practice out of reach. When your records sit on a box in your town, held by a cooperative in your region, your rights are the laws of your own country—and your own courts can hear a complaint, including breaches of rights.

Local law also answers a quieter problem: a dispute is only as good as the evidence behind it. Records held about people today are rarely kept in a form a court could rely on—unsigned, unprovenanced, quietly alterable. The cooperative's records are kept evidence-grade: signed, timestamped and provenance-tracked, so a claim can be proven rather than merely asserted.
